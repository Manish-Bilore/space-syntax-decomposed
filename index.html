Which design choices make Space Syntax differ from street-network analysis, by how much, and where? Copenhagen and Mumbai.

Space Syntax and street-network analysis rank the same streets differently. We take the route from one to the other apart, one design choice at a time, and measure what each choice does, and where.

In short

We ran Space Syntax (depthmapX) and street-network analysis (cityseer) on the same streets of Copenhagen and Mumbai. Most of the gap between them comes from how the data are processed: graph type, cost, normalisation, network cleaning and map extent. Normalisation is the largest single step, and network cleaning alone moves agreement as much as switching from metres to turns.

Both tools describe network configuration only. depthmapX was built to analyse the spatial layout of buildings and street systems as a proxy for movement potential; cityseer to compute localised centralities and land-use accessibility on street networks. Neither observes movement, so the next phase adds public transport, pedestrian counts and land-use data to test which measure, if any, predicts how people actually move.

Six findings

Rank agreement (Spearman ρ) between measures over interior streets, both cities.

ρ ≈ 0

Closeness and NAIN disagree

At 400 m, metric closeness and normalised angular integration (NAIN) are unrelated in both cities. Normalisation is the largest single step.

0.6–0.8

Choice is robust

Axial choice tracks normalised angular choice (NACH) at every radius in both cities; axial integration does not (0.1–0.45).

4.2×

Cleaning is a model choice

Junction consolidation changes Copenhagen's street count 4.2× and moves engine agreement as much as angular cost.

0.83

Position beats size

A larger map with Dharavi at its edge ranks it worse (ρ 0.83) than a 1 km centred surround (0.96).

≤ 0.28

Generated lines fall short of axial maps

Axial lines generated from OSM do not reproduce a hand-drawn map, whatever the input or tolerance.

≤ 0.04

Neighbourhoods over cities

City medians differ by at most 0.04; 1 km tiles within one city span 0.20–0.60.
